## Current Work

### Freshwater Mussel Genetic Resource Repository

My main project at the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History is establishing and growing a centralized collection of freshwater mussel genetic resources in collaboration with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. The work connects curated museum specimens with tissues, nonlethal swabs, DNA, and their associated provenance and collection data, preserving resources that can support research over time.

Part of this work is preserving material for different kinds of future research, including flash-frozen tissues suitable for high-molecular-weight DNA and genomic applications.

### Supporting Freshwater Mussel Genomics

I support freshwater mussel research projects. One is a pilot using Oxford Nanopore long-read sequencing to develop a reference genome for Lampsilis rafinesqueana and test an approach that could be extended to other freshwater mussels.

I also contribute laboratory and computational work to 3RAD-seq analyses of wild and propagated populations, and computational analyses to a study of the evolution of cementation in freshwater mussels (manuscript submitted).

## Past Projects

### Single-Copy Sequencing and Viral Evolution

At the NIH Vaccine Research Center, I worked across the laboratory and computational sides of high-throughput viral sequencing. I adapted a UMI-based single-copy sequencing approach previously used with HIV and SARS-CoV-2 for long-read PacBio sequencing of influenza, bringing an emerging sequencing approach into a new viral system. We used it to examine H5N1 variation and evolution in a universal influenza vaccine study.

### Fast-Evolving Genes

My work graduate work at Simon Fraser University explored why some genes evolve so quickly, particularly those involved in reproduction. Echinoderms gave us plenty to work with, but the questions also took us into vertebrates. Along the way, I worked with genomic, transcriptomic, population genetic, and simulated data to look at evolution.
